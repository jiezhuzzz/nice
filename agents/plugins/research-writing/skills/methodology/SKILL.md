# Research Methodology Writing

## Overview
Use this skill to help a researcher write a clear scientific Methodology section. The section should let readers repeat the work and obtain comparable results, but it should also persuade readers that the chosen materials, procedures, equipment, and conditions were appropriate and handled carefully.

The guidance is adapted from Unit 2, "Writing about Methodology," of *Science Research Writing: For Non-Native Speakers of English* by Hilary Glasman-Deal.

## First Checks
Before drafting or revising, identify:
- target section name: Methodology, Materials and Methods, Experimental, Procedure, Simulation, Model, or another journal-specific label
- journal or supervisor requirements about active/passive voice, tense, and level of detail
- whether the work is experimental, computational, observational, field-based, or mixed
- materials, samples, equipment, software, locations, quantities, conditions, duration, sequence, and analysis methods
- which procedures are standard, modified from prior work, or novel
- choices that need justification
- problems, approximations, contamination risks, missing data, or limitations that affect interpretation

If information is missing, ask only for details that affect reproducibility or credibility. Do not invent methods, quantities, equipment models, or results.

## Core Model
The Methodology model is a menu. Select the components needed for the user's field and journal.

1. Provide a general introduction and overview.
   - State the purpose, scope, number of samples/tests/models/sites, or overall procedure.
   - Give the source of materials, samples, equipment, software, or data.
   - Supply essential background information about materials, locations, instruments, or standard methods.
   - Restate the purpose of the work if it helps the reader enter the section smoothly.

2. Provide specific and precise details.
   - Include quantities, temperatures, times, sizes, locations, sequence, conditions, software versions, instrument settings, and analysis parameters.
   - Justify significant choices.
   - Indicate that appropriate care was taken through precise descriptions and careful adverb/adjective use.

3. Relate materials or methods to other studies.
   - Say whether the method was identical to, adapted from, or significantly different from a cited method.
   - Place citations where they support the exact procedure or claim.
   - If the method was modified, state the modification when it matters.

4. Indicate where problems occurred.
   - Mention methodological difficulties early when they affect interpretation.
   - Minimize responsibly: state the problem, its likely effect, and any control or mitigation.
   - Do not save important method limitations for the Discussion as a surprise.

## Drafting Process
1. Check the target journal or thesis rules before choosing active/passive voice.
2. Begin top-down with an overview when the procedure is complex or the readership is broad.
3. Break the overview into precise procedural details in logical order.
4. Justify choices that readers might question, especially sampling, materials, controls, locations, instruments, parameter values, and deviations from standard methods.
5. Use references as shortcuts for standard procedures, but include enough detail for professional courtesy and reproducibility.
6. Distinguish clearly between standard methods, previous researchers' methods, and what was done in the present study.
7. Mention problems or approximations only when relevant, and explain why they do not invalidate the work or how they will be addressed.

## Passive, Active, And Tense
Most Methodology sections use agentless passive constructions, but some fields and journals allow active voice.

Use tense to distinguish meanings:
- Present Simple passive: standard procedures, normal operation, generally true equipment behavior, or procedures described in a figure/model.
- Past Simple passive: what was done in the present study.
- Active "we": acceptable in many journal articles when the author team performed the action and the target journal allows it.
- Avoid "I" in thesis-style methodology unless the institution explicitly allows it.

Make ownership visible when needed:
- Present study: "In this study...", "In our experiments...", "This procedure..."
- Previous work: "In Brown (1999)...", "using the method of Brown et al.", "as described by..."

This distinction matters because readers must know which choices and innovations belong to the current work.

## Articles: A, The, And No Article
Check article use carefully, especially for non-native English writing.

- Use `the` when both writer and reader know which item is meant, or when there is only one possible referent.
- Use `a/an` when introducing a countable item that is new to the reader or when the exact item does not matter.
- Use no article for plural or uncountable general reference where appropriate.
- Sound controls `a/an`: "an MRI scan" but "a UV light."

Article choice can change meaning. "A connection" means one possible connection; "the connection" means a known or assumed connection.

## Adverbs And Precision
Use adverbs to clarify care and procedure, but avoid ambiguity.

Helpful care markers include: accurately, carefully, directly, exactly, freshly, gently, immediately, independently, individually, precisely, randomly, reliably, separately, suitably, tightly, thoroughly, uniformly.

Place adverbs where they attach to the intended action. If a sentence has multiple adverbs or could be misread, split it into shorter units or move sentence-level adverbs to the front.

## Useful Language Patterns
Use conventional phrases and adapt them to the user's field.

### Overview, Materials, And Sources
- "The current investigation involved..."
- "A total of X samples was/were collected..."
- "All reactions were performed..."
- "The substrate was obtained from..."
- "The equipment was supplied by..."
- "The experiments were conducted at..."
- "The material investigated was..."

### Spatial And Equipment Description
- "was located downstream of..."
- "was mounted on..."
- "was aligned with..."
- "was positioned adjacent to..."
- "was connected to..."
- "was embedded in..."
- "was fixed to..."

### Specific Procedures
- "was adapted", "was added", "was calibrated", "was collected", "was constructed", "was extracted", "was filtered", "was measured", "was modified", "was prepared", "was recorded", "was repeated", "was sampled", "was simulated", "was treated", "was varied"

### Justifying Choices
- "in order to..."
- "so as to..."
- "for the purpose of..."
- "selected on the basis of..."
- "to take advantage of..."
- "which allowed..."
- "thereby reducing..."
- "for the sake of simplicity..."

### Relating To Previous Studies
- Identical: "as described by...", "according to...", "using the method of...", "as reported previously"
- Similar: "a modified version of...", "adapted from...", "based on...", "slightly modified", "with some adjustments"
- Significantly different: "loosely based on...", "with the following modifications...", "a novel step was introduced..."

### Problems And Limitations
- "Only approximate..."
- "less than ideal..."
- "a minor deficit..."
- "negligible..."
- "Although X could not remove all Y, the amount remaining was negligible."
- "Future work will..." for the author's own planned work.
- "Future work should..." for a general direction the field could take.

## Detail Level
Include enough detail for replication:
- exact sample counts and selection criteria
- material sources and grades when relevant
- instrument names, models, settings, and calibration when relevant
- software, algorithms, and parameter values
- sequence of steps and timing
- environmental and experimental conditions
- controls, replicates, randomization, exclusion criteria, and contamination prevention

Do not over-discuss results here. Mention preliminary results only when they shaped the design or demonstrate that a methodological problem did not affect the results.

## Quality Checklist
Before returning a Methodology section, check:
- The section has enough detail for replication.
- The opening gives a helpful overview if the method is complex.
- Standard procedures are separated from what the present study did.
- Previous methods are cited accurately and modifications are explicit.
- Significant choices are justified.
- Care and quality control are visible without sounding defensive.
- Tense choices distinguish standard procedure from present work.
- Article use (`a`, `the`, no article) does not change meaning accidentally.
- Adverbs are placed unambiguously.
- Limitations or problems are acknowledged when they matter.
- Results are not discussed in detail before the Results section.

## Output Formats
For a new draft, provide:
1. `Methodology draft`
2. `Model map` showing which paragraphs or sentences perform components 1-4
3. `Missing details to verify` for any reproducibility-critical facts

For revision, provide:
1. revised methodology text
2. concise notes on reproducibility, justification, tense/voice, articles, and ambiguity

For diagnosis or outline, use the four-component model as the organizing frame.
