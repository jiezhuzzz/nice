# Research Introduction Writing

## Overview
Use this skill to help a researcher write a conventional scientific Introduction that guides readers from the broad research field to the specific study. The Introduction should not start with the user's experiment or aim too abruptly; it should establish why the field matters, locate the work in previous research, create a research space, and then describe the present paper.

The guidance is adapted from Unit 1, "How to Write an Introduction," of *Science Research Writing: For Non-Native Speakers of English* by Hilary Glasman-Deal.

## First Checks
Before drafting or revising, identify:
- target genre: journal article, thesis chapter, conference paper, proposal, or class assignment
- approximate length and journal or supervisor requirements
- title, keywords, and intended readership
- field significance and essential background facts
- key previous studies and how they should be grouped
- research gap, problem, question, or prediction
- what the present paper does, including method and main finding only if appropriate

If details are missing, draft from the user's notes without inventing citations or results. Use placeholders only where the user clearly needs to supply a fact, reference, or claim.

## Core Model
Most research Introductions follow four broad moves. Use this model to outline, draft, diagnose, or reorder text.

1. Establish the importance of the field.
   - State why the topic is significant, useful, common, economically important, technically challenging, or currently active.
   - Provide general background facts or define terms from the title.
   - Move from general information to more specific information.
   - Present the broad problem area or current research focus.

2. Present previous and current research.
   - Give a brief, organized overview of key studies and contributions.
   - Choose an ordering principle: chronological development, competing approaches/models, or general-to-specific movement.
   - Mention only studies that help draw the research map leading to the present work.

3. Locate the gap or problem.
   - Show what remains incomplete, unclear, inefficient, untested, limited, or unresolved.
   - Use respectful, impersonal criticism.
   - Prefer statements such as "few studies have addressed..." or "however, little attention has been paid to..." over direct attacks on researchers.
   - If relevant, present a prediction or hypothesis to be tested.

4. Describe the present paper.
   - State the purpose, focus, contribution, method, structure, or scope of the paper.
   - Give methodology or findings only in enough detail to orient the reader; leave full details for Methodology and Results.
   - End the Introduction by interfacing cleanly with the central report sections.

## Drafting Process
1. Start with a "meeting place" fact that many intended readers can share.
2. Narrow gradually toward the specific system, material, method, population, theory, or problem.
3. Use citations both for background facts and for the short literature review, but do not turn the opening into an unordered list of papers.
4. Make each paragraph begin with a topic sentence that previews the paragraph's main idea.
5. Connect sentences explicitly so readers can follow the logic.
6. State the research gap before describing the present paper.
7. Finish with a present-work paragraph that tells readers what this paper does.

For a first practice draft, follow the model closely. For experienced users, adapt it to the target journal while preserving the reader's path from general field to present paper.

## Tense Guidance
Tense choices carry meaning in Introductions.

- Present Simple: accepted facts, definitions, current truths, and claims the writer presents as established.
- Present Perfect: research activity that remains relevant now, especially with phrases like "in recent years" or "over the past decade."
- Past Simple: specific completed studies, findings, experiments, or contributions by named researchers.
- Present Simple for the paper itself: "This paper presents...", "This study focuses on..."
- Past Simple for the project aim when treating the aim as prior to the completed study: "The aim of this study was..."

Do not change tense randomly. A shift from Past Simple to Present Perfect can signal that a gap remains relevant now; a shift from Past Simple to Present Simple can strengthen a finding by presenting it as an accepted fact.

## Signalling And Flow
Close the gap between sentences. Every sentence should make its relationship to the previous sentence clear through at least one of these methods:

- overlap: repeat an important term from the previous sentence
- pronoun or pro-form: "this approach," "these systems," "this method"
- semicolon or relative clause when ideas are very closely related
- signalling connector

Common connector roles:
- Cause: because, since, due to, in view of
- Result: therefore, consequently, hence, as a result
- Contrast: however, whereas, while, by contrast, on the other hand
- Unexpectedness: although, despite, nevertheless, yet
- Addition: in addition, moreover, furthermore, also

Use connectors precisely. Avoid starting formal research prose with "so" to signal a result.

## Active, Passive, And Paper Subjects
Use "we" only when it clearly refers to the author team, not to people in general. For general knowledge, prefer "It is known that..." or a factual construction.

In thesis writing, avoid "I" unless the institution explicitly allows it. Use passive constructions, "this study," "this paper," "the present work," or "here" to identify the author's work.

In the final present-work move, a clear dummy subject often works better than an agentless passive:
- "This paper presents..."
- "The present study investigates..."
- "Here we examine..."
- "This article describes..."

## Paragraphing
Use paragraphs as visual and logical guides.

- Avoid clusters of one-sentence paragraphs.
- Avoid very long paragraphs that contain several unrelated ideas.
- Begin most paragraphs with a topic sentence.
- Start a new paragraph when the topic, role, or stage of the model changes.
- Plan paragraphs by listing the idea for each paragraph and bulleting the supporting points under it.

## Useful Language Patterns
Use conventional phrases and adapt them to the user's field.

### Establishing Significance
- "X plays a key role in..."
- "X has attracted much attention in recent years..."
- "There is considerable interest in..."
- "X is a major issue in..."
- "Recent studies have focused on..."
- "X is widely recognized as..."

### Previous Research
- "A number of studies have investigated..."
- "Smith et al. demonstrated that..."
- "Previous work has focused on..."
- "Early studies established..."
- "More recently, X showed..."
- "An alternative approach was developed by..."

### Gap, Problem, Or Question
- "However, few studies have addressed..."
- "Nevertheless, little attention has been paid to..."
- "The function of X remains unclear."
- "There remains a need for..."
- "Although this approach improves X, it does not..."
- "Previous work has been confined to..."

When criticizing previous work, soften strong claims if needed: "may not always be completely successful" is often safer than "is unsuccessful."

### Present Work
- "This paper focuses on..."
- "The purpose of this study is to..."
- "In this paper we present..."
- "The present study investigates..."
- "This paper introduces a method that..."
- "This study tests the hypothesis that..."
- "This paper is organized as follows..." only when the journal or paper length makes a roadmap useful.

## Quality Checklist
Before returning an Introduction, check:
- It begins with field significance or shared background, not the user's specific experiment.
- General background narrows toward the user's specific research area.
- Prior work is grouped logically rather than listed randomly.
- Citations are placed where they support the exact claim.
- The research gap is explicit, respectful, and specific.
- The present-work sentence clearly states what the paper does.
- Tense choices match the function of each sentence.
- Sentence connectors show cause, contrast, result, addition, or unexpectedness accurately.
- Paragraphs have topic sentences and coherent scope.
- Methods and results are not over-explained before their own sections.

## Output Formats
For a new draft, provide:
1. `Introduction draft`
2. `Model map` showing which sentences or paragraphs perform moves 1-4
3. `Notes` with missing citations, facts, or claims the user should verify

For revision, provide:
1. revised introduction
2. concise notes on structure, gap statement, flow, tense, and citations

For diagnosis or outline, use the four-move model as the organizing frame.
