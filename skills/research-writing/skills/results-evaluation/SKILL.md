# Research Results Evaluation Writing

## Overview
Use this skill to help a researcher write a Results or Data Analysis section that does more than repeat numbers from tables and figures. The section should guide readers through the findings, evaluate what the results mean, compare them with prior work or predictions when useful, acknowledge problems, and begin moving toward implications without turning into a full Discussion.

The guidance is adapted from Unit 3, "Writing about Results," of *Science Research Writing: For Non-Native Speakers of English* by Hilary Glasman-Deal.

## First Checks
Before drafting or revising, identify:
- target section label: Results, Data Analysis, Analysis, Results and Discussion, or another journal-specific heading
- whether the journal separates Results from Discussion or combines them
- research aim, original prediction, or hypothesis
- key methods needed to interpret the results
- tables, figures, models, equations, or images to reference
- which findings are key, typical, unusual, problematic, or peripheral
- relevant prior studies, predictions, or model outputs for comparison
- the level of certainty appropriate for interpretations and causal claims

If the user provides raw values without context, do not merely narrate every number. Ask or infer which results answer the research aim, then organize around those results.

## Core Model
The Results model is a menu. Select the components needed for the user's data and target journal.

1. Revisit the research aim, existing research, methodology, or general results pattern.
   - Briefly remind readers what question, prediction, method, or previous finding the results address.
   - Give a general overview before individual results when the section is complex.
   - Repeat this overview move when shifting to a new set of results.

2. Present and evaluate specific/key results.
   - Invite readers to view tables, figures, equations, or images at the right moment.
   - Describe selected key results in detail.
   - Use evaluative language to tell readers what the values mean.
   - Compare results with prior studies, model predictions, simulations, or expected outcomes.
   - Add direct, limited explanation when needed to make a result understandable.

3. Address problems with results.
   - Mention incomplete, inconsistent, unexpected, or less reliable results when they matter.
   - Minimize responsibly by stating scope, likely reasons, and practical impact.
   - Do not hide problems that informed readers will notice.

4. State possible implications.
   - Near the end of the section, say what the results suggest, indicate, imply, or make possible.
   - Keep broad interpretation for the Discussion unless the journal combines Results and Discussion.

## Drafting Process
1. Start with an overview or a link back to the aim, prediction, method, or previous research.
2. Decide which results deserve detailed text. Do not give every value equal attention.
3. Present results in an order that helps readers understand the logic, not necessarily in the order they were obtained.
4. Use figure and table references strategically. If the data could be interpreted in several ways, comment before inviting readers to inspect the figure.
5. Add evaluative wording so numbers are interpreted rather than left as "naked numbers."
6. Compare with prior work or predictions where this helps locate the results on the research map.
7. Discuss result problems briefly and professionally.
8. End by pointing toward possible implications or applications.

## Sequence, Frequency, Quantity, And Causality
Use these language choices to control how readers understand the evidence.

### Sequence
Use precise sequence language rather than only "then" or "next":
- before the work: beforehand, previously, prior to, in advance
- beginning: initially, at the beginning, to begin with
- order: after, subsequently, once, then
- short interval: soon, shortly after, before long
- later stage: eventually, later, towards the end
- simultaneous events: while, when, at the same time, simultaneously, immediately
- end: finally, lastly, at the end
- after the end: afterwards, later on, in the end

### Frequency
Frequency affects reliability. Choose terms carefully:
- 100%: always, invariably, without exception
- high: generally, normally, usually, regularly, frequently, often
- neutral: as often as not
- low: sometimes, occasionally, rarely, seldom, hardly ever
- zero: never, on no occasion, not once

Frequency terms can be subjective. A result that occurs in 18% of cases may be "frequent" if the expected rate was near zero, but "rare" if the expected rate was high.

### Quantity And Evaluation
Quantity language can increase, reduce, emphasize, approximate, or avoid overcommitting:
- stronger/larger: considerable, substantial, marked, as many as, over, significant
- weaker/smaller: only, barely, negligible, slight, marginal, few
- emphasis: considerably, substantially, exceptionally, far above, well under
- closeness: approximately, nearly, close to, virtually, just over
- cautious evaluation: fairly, relatively, somewhat, in some cases

Use evaluative phrases when they add meaning: "only 23%" and "as many as 23%" lead readers to different interpretations.

### Causality
Do not overclaim causal relationships. Choose the strength of claim:
- strong: caused, produced, resulted in, was due to
- partial: contributed to, was a factor in, influenced
- connected but weaker: was associated with, was linked to, was related to
- cautious framing: "The evidence suggests that...", "It appears that...", "It may be inferred that...", "This implies that..."

Use `result in` and `result from` carefully: `x results in y` means y follows from x; `x results from y` means x follows from y.

## Figure And Table References
Use varied and accurate figure/table language:
- "Figure 1 shows..."
- "Table 2 summarizes..."
- "The results are given in..."
- "As can be seen from Fig. 1..."
- "Inspection of Fig. 1 indicates..."
- "The data in Fig. 1 suggest..."
- "Comparing Figs. 1 and 4 shows..."

Use `from` when the reader can deduce something from the figure/table. Use `in` when the information visibly appears in the figure/table.

## Comparing With Other Results
Use comparison to show where the findings fit:
- consistent with, in line with, in good agreement with
- compare well with, correlate with, match
- confirm, support, reinforce, validate
- inconsistent with, contrary to, unlike, refute

Place citations precisely. A reference at the end of the sentence may incorrectly make prior researchers seem to own the current result.

## Handling Problems
Research is not invalidated by imperfect results when the problems are presented professionally.

Use language that acknowledges, limits, and explains:
- "It should be noted that..."
- "Although X was less than ideal..."
- "The mismatch was slight..."
- "This effect was local/negligible/of little significance..."
- "This may have been due to..."
- "This was not within the scope of the present study."
- "Future work will..." for the author's own planned work.
- "Future work should..." for a broader recommendation to the field.

Mention important problems in Results, not only later as future work.

## Results Versus Discussion
In a separate Results section:
- focus on what was found, what selected results mean, and direct comparisons
- keep explanations close to the data
- use implications as a transition near the end

In a combined Results and Discussion section:
- include broader explanations, literature positioning, and implications as needed
- still keep each result close to the evidence that supports it

## Quality Checklist
Before returning a Results/evaluation section, check:
- It does not merely repeat table or figure values.
- It starts with an overview or link to aim, method, prediction, or prior research.
- Key results are selected and emphasized; peripheral results are not over-described.
- Figure/table invitations are placed after interpretation when the display is ambiguous.
- Frequency and quantity language accurately reflects the data and intended strength.
- Comparisons with prior studies or predictions are clear and cited precisely.
- Causal language is appropriately strong or cautious.
- Problematic results are acknowledged if they matter.
- The ending points toward implications without overextending beyond the evidence.

## Output Formats
For a new draft, provide:
1. `Results/evaluation draft`
2. `Model map` showing which paragraphs or sentences perform components 1-4
3. `Checks or missing details` for data, figure/table labels, citations, or claims that need verification

For revision, provide:
1. revised Results/evaluation text
2. concise notes on result selection, evaluative language, comparisons, causality, and problem handling

For diagnosis or outline, use the four-component model as the organizing frame.
