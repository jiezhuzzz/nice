# Research Abstract Writing

## Overview
Use this skill to help a researcher turn paper content into a clear, journal-ready scientific abstract and, when requested, a precise research title. The abstract should stand alone: many readers will see it in a database before deciding whether to read the full paper.

The guidance is adapted from Unit 5, "Writing the Abstract," of *Science Research Writing: For Non-Native Speakers of English* by Hilary Glasman-Deal.

## First Checks
Before drafting, identify:
- target journal requirements, especially word limit and whether the abstract must be structured with headings
- intended reader and field-specific keywords
- the paper's aim, method/materials, key results, contribution, implications, and any necessary limitation
- whether the user wants a full draft, revision, diagnosis, title, or multiple options

If the journal format is unknown, default to a single-paragraph, results-focused abstract of 150-200 words unless the user asks otherwise.

## Abstract Models
Choose the model that matches the journal and paper.

### Model 1: Structured Summary
Use this when the journal requires headings or when the paper needs balanced coverage of background, aim, method, results, and conclusion.

Typical order:
1. Background, aim, problem, or what the paper does
2. Methodology or materials
3. Results, achievement, contribution, or implications
4. Applications, if important
5. Limitations or future work, only if necessary

Keep background brief. If readers need context, use one or two sentences and move quickly to method and results.

### Model 2: Results-Focused Abstract
Use this as the default for most research articles, especially narrow or specialized studies where the audience already knows the background.

Select two or three components from the Model 1 list, usually method plus results or contribution. Combine components in single sentences where possible:
- what the paper does + method/materials + aim
- problem/gap + achievement
- method + numerical result
- result + implication/application

This model is often short. It still needs enough method and result detail for a reader to decide whether the article is relevant.

## Drafting Process
1. Do not invent content. Derive the abstract from the finished paper, notes, or user-provided data.
2. Select the model based on journal rules and research focus.
3. Draft without worrying about the word limit.
4. Cut anything that is not essential to reader selection: broad background, repeated aim statements, excessive methods detail, unexplained caveats, and claims not supported by the paper.
5. Check that the abstract is self-contained and understandable without the paper.
6. Add exact search keywords and define acronyms or specialized terms the first time they appear.
7. Verify that the final version stays within the word limit.

## Tense Guidance
Use tense to signal the role of each statement:

- Present Simple: general problem, known background, what the paper does, what the paper contains, strong results, applications, and broadly valid implications.
- Past Simple: what was done during the study and most specific experimental observations.
- Present Perfect: achievements that connect past work to current significance.

Mixed tense is acceptable when it carries meaning. For example, the study may have found a result in the past, while the result itself can be stated in the present if the writer presents it as a current fact.

## Detail Level
Prefer specific, interpretable information:
- Include key numbers when they are central to the result.
- Avoid vague words such as "better," "small," "various," or "significant" unless the sentence also explains the basis.
- When giving numbers, add context so the value cannot be misunderstood.
- Include methodology detail when the method is the contribution or when readers need it to judge relevance.
- Mention limitations or future work only when they are important to interpreting the paper.

## Language Patterns
Use conventional, direct scientific phrasing. Keep the abstract slightly less technical than the full paper when that helps database readers, but preserve necessary field terms and keywords.

Useful sentence roles:
- Background/problem: "A need remains for...", "However, existing approaches...", "Recent studies have..."
- Aim/paper action: "This study investigates...", "This paper presents...", "We examine...", "We propose..."
- Method: "Samples were prepared...", "The data were evaluated...", "A model was constructed..."
- Results: "It was found that...", "The treatment increased...", "The model produced...", "No effect was observed..."
- Contribution: "This approach provides...", "The results demonstrate...", "The method enables..."
- Implication/application: "These results suggest...", "This framework can be used...", "The approach is suitable for..."

Do not over-soften the abstract. Weak phrases such as "seems to suggest" often belong in discussion, not in a concise abstract, unless the evidence is genuinely preliminary.

## Title Guidance
When asked to create or revise a title:
- start from the research question, aim, or main result
- include searchable keywords
- use sentence case unless the target journal requires otherwise
- keep the title concise and neutral
- avoid "A study of..." and "An investigation into..." unless the journal style clearly allows it
- avoid unclear compound nouns and preposition-heavy phrasing
- do not use evaluative words such as "reliable" unless the paper directly establishes that claim
- if the study is limited to specific materials, settings, systems, organisms, or cases, make that scope visible

## Quality Checklist
Before returning the abstract or title, check:
- The model fits the journal or user request.
- The abstract can stand alone.
- The main method and main result are present.
- Key numeric results are included when they matter.
- Claims are supported by the provided paper content.
- Important acronyms or technical terms are defined.
- Search keywords are present naturally.
- Tenses consistently signal background, study actions, results, and implications.
- The final text meets the word limit.

## Output Formats
For a new draft, provide:
1. `Abstract` with word count
2. `Why this structure works` in 2-4 bullets, unless the user asks only for final text
3. `Optional title` if the user also needs one or the current title seems weak

For revision, provide:
1. revised abstract with word count
2. concise notes on the most important changes
3. unresolved questions only if missing information prevents a reliable abstract

For diagnosis, use the quality checklist and give concrete fixes.
